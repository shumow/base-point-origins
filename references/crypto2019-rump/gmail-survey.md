**1. Research question**  
For each relevant elliptic curve in SEC 2, take its published base point `G` and compute the unique half within the odd-order cyclic subgroup generated by `G`:  
\[ H = \[(n+1)/2\]G, \qquad \[2\]H=G, \]  
where `n` is the (odd) order of `G`. Then inspect the canonical nonnegative integer representative of the affine `x` coordinate of `H` and compare its bit length with the field degree / bit length. In binary fields the coordinate representation depends on the specified polynomial basis; the meaning of 'short' should be fixed before cross-curve comparisons.  
This is a **group-theoretic half**, not ordinary integer halving of either coordinate. On a full curve with even group order, there may be other preimages of `G` under doubling, but the subgroup half above is canonical.

    **3. Mathematical interpretation**  
    For odd `n`, `2` is invertible modulo `n`, so `k=(n+1)//2` satisfies `2k ``≡`` 1 (mod n)`. Consequently `H=kG` is uniquely determined within `<G>`.  
    Define `b=bit_length(x(H))` using the canonical integer encoding of the field element, and `m` as the coordinate width. A short coordinate has `m-b` leading zero bits. If an x-coordinate were uniformly distributed over `m`-bit strings, the rough probability of at least `t` leading zeros would be `2^-t`; for real curves the exact distribution and conditioning matter. Testing many curves, multiples, encodings, or candidate definitions of 'short' introduces a look-elsewhere effect.  
    **Security interpretation:** a small `x(H)` does not by itself reveal the discrete logarithm of `G`, because the relation `G=2H` is already public and trivial. It could nevertheless be interesting for generator provenance or deliberate parameter selection. Any stronger inference requires a specified null model and a comparison with the standards-generation process.
