Experiment 2: high-bit (top-byte / prefix) analysis of the secp*k1 half-generators.

Core shared by all four: True
Byte above the core: secp160k1=48, secp192k1=78, secp224k1=78, secp256k1=78
  192-minus-160 of that byte: 0x30
  prefix 3b78 shared by 192/224/256: True

(C) single-value-reduced-mod-p matches (item 3d):
  non-trivial (actual reduction): none
  (secp224k1 and secp256k1 have identical half-x, so they match each other at quotient 0; that is the known identity, not a reduction.)

(D) is the observed byte-above-core the smallest leaving (core|nibble) on the curve?
  secp160k1: observed 48 (on-curve True), smallest on-curve 04, observed-is-smallest False
  secp192k1: observed 78 (on-curve True), smallest on-curve 00, observed-is-smallest False
  secp224k1: observed 78 (on-curve True), smallest on-curve 01, observed-is-smallest False
  secp256k1: observed 78 (on-curve True), smallest on-curve 01, observed-is-smallest False

Conclusion (negative). The byte above the core is 48 on secp160k1 and 78 on the other three (difference exactly 0x30), and 192's prefix ends in the 3b78 shared by 224/256 -- structural facts, not a derivation. No non-trivial single-value mod-p reduction relates the curves, and the observed top byte, while always on-curve, is the smallest on-curve completion for none of them. So the high bits are not explained by a simple counter, a single mod-p reduction, or a smallest-on-curve rule. The prefix's origin stays open; next are the item 3b/3c hash/expansion stories and the archival route.
